# Chapter Analysis Blueprint
## A systematic framework for assessing consistency across layers

This blueprint is designed to be applied to each chapter individually, then across all chapters as a cross-check. Work through each layer in order. The layers build on each other — problems in Layer 1 (physical) often reveal problems in Layer 4 (sound).

---

## How to use this

For each chapter, work through the five layers below. For each layer, answer every question. Mark issues as:
- **[C]** — Contradiction (something conflicts with itself or another chapter)
- **[G]** — Gap (something missing that should be there)
- **[O]** — Overreach (something that says too much, explains what the reader/listener already understands)
- **[T]** — Tone break (a line that belongs to a different kind of text — essay, lecture, journalism — rather than the narrator's voice)

After assessing all layers for a single chapter, do the **Seams** check at the end — that's where cross-chapter problems live.

---

## Layer 1: The Body (Physical Journey)

The narrator is a body moving through space. Every chapter should be answerable on the level of: where is this person, what are they doing, what can they physically perceive?

1. **Where is the narrator?** Can you point to a location? Is it established in the content, or only in the TLDR/soundlayer?
2. **What is the narrator's body doing?** Sitting, walking, touching, holding something? Is there a physical action or posture?
3. **What can the narrator perceive from this position?** What is visible, audible, tactile? Does the text claim perceptions that don't fit the location?
4. **Is there movement?** If yes — from where to where? Is the transition marked or does the narrator teleport?
5. **Does the physical situation change during the chapter?** If yes — what causes the change? Is it triggered by something internal (a thought) or external (a sound, a vibration, someone speaking)?
6. **Continuity with previous chapter:** Where did the narrator end up at the close of the previous chapter? Does this chapter pick up from there, or is there a gap? If there's a gap, is it deliberate or accidental?

### What to watch for:
- Location established only in metadata (TLDR, soundlayer) but not in the content itself
- Physical details that appear mid-chapter without being planted earlier (e.g., a playground appearing in a closing paragraph when no playground was mentioned)
- The narrator perceiving things they couldn't from their stated position

---

## Layer 2: The Mind (Intellectual Journey)

The narrator thinks. Each chapter moves from observation to reflection to theory to case study and back. The intellectual movement should feel like thought — associative, sometimes surprising — not like an essay outline.

1. **What triggers the thinking?** Every intellectual passage should have a sensory or situational trigger. What is it? 
2. **What is the central intellectual move?** Each chapter makes one main argument or observation. What is it? Can you state it in one sentence without using the word "explores"?
3. **How does the chapter move between observation and theory?** Map the transitions: body → thought → theory → case → body. Are the transitions motivated (one thing leads to another) or arbitrary (topic change without bridge)?
4. **Does the narrator stay inside or step outside?** When describing systems, does the narrator describe them as someone affected, or as someone lecturing? 
5. **Are case studies earning their place?** Each real-world example should do work in the chapter's argument. Does it advance the thought, or is it listed for completeness?
6. **Does the chapter end where it started — but changed?** The physical situation should return but the narrator's understanding should have shifted.

### What to watch for:
- Theory arriving without a sensory trigger 
- Case studies that repeat each other's point 
- The narrator suddenly knowing things they couldn't know from their position
- Punchlines that summarize what the reader already understood

---

## Layer 3: The Voice (Tone and Register)

This is an audiopiece. The narrator's voice — delivered by a cloned TTS — is the listener's only guide. The register must stay consistent within each chapter and evolve credibly across the piece.

1. **Who is speaking?** First person ("I"), second person ("you"), or a mix? If mixed — are the shifts motivated, or does the narrator slip into "you" when making general claims?
2. **What is the register?** Conversational? Reflective? Essayistic? Testimony? Each chapter can have its own register, but it shouldn't shift without reason within a chapter.
3. **Read any sentence aloud.** Does it sound like someone thinking, or like someone writing? Sentences that work on the page may not work as spoken audio. Watch for:
   - Sentences too long to say in one breath
   - Clauses nested more than two deep
   - Words chosen for their look rather than their sound
4. **Is the narrator trustworthy?** Not morally — perceptually. Does the narrator claim to feel things that sound performed? Does the narrator deliver judgments that sound too certain for the situation?
5. **Rhetorical devices:** Count parallel constructions ("The X doesn't Y. The Z doesn't W."). If a pattern appears more than twice in a chapter, it's becoming a tic. Once across the whole piece is a motif; five times is a crutch.
6. **The TTS trap:** Remember that a cloned voice is delivering this. Lines that describe artificial systems "performing care" or "sounding human" will land differently when spoken by an artificial voice. This is a strength — but only if the text doesn't over-explain it. Check: does the text trust the listener to feel the irony, or does it underline it?

### What to watch for:
- Editorial punchlines ("None of this is a flaw," "The absurdity of it is total") — these belong in opinion pieces, not in a narrator's inner voice
- Shifts from "I" to "you" that aren't motivated by a change in what the narrator is doing
- Sentences that explain what an image already showed
- The word "just" used to create false simplicity ("It just moves," "Just — immediately")

---

## Layer 4: The Sound (Soundlayer Consistency)

The soundlayer is not a soundtrack. It is a parallel narrative. It must be consistent with the content, evolve across chapters, and never illustrate what the text already says.

1. **What is the primary sound world?** Organic (field recording), digital (synth), institutional (bureaucratic hum), human (voices), or hybrid? Does this match the chapter's position in the overall arc?
2. **Sound arc across chapters — does this chapter fit?**

3. **Does the soundlayer contradict the content?** If the content says "vibration" but the soundlayer says "tone" — that's a contradiction. If the content describes silence but the soundlayer describes ambient noise — check whether that's intentional layering or a mistake.
4. **Diegetic vs. non-diegetic:** Which sounds exist in the narrator's world (diegetic) and which exist only for the listener (non-diegetic)? Is this distinction clear? Are there moments where the boundary is deliberately blurred — and if so, is the blurring doing work?
5. **Sound cues at transitions:** When the narrator's state changes, does the soundlayer respond? The sound should shift at these moments — not to illustrate the action, but to mark the change in the narrator's attention.
6. **Does the soundlayer do something the text doesn't?** The best moments are when sound carries meaning the text leaves unsaid (e.g., unfamiliar music bleeding in at "someone else's child on a swing" — the text doesn't mention music, but the listener hears it). Check: is there at least one moment per chapter where the soundlayer adds a layer the text doesn't name?

### What to watch for:
- Soundlayer that merely illustrates the text (narrator says "birds" → birds in soundlayer). The sound should add, not double.
- Missing sound cues at critical transitions
- Soundlayer descriptions that contradict the content's sensory claims
- The TTS voice's own artificiality not being accounted for in chapters where it should create tension (especially Ch4 and Ch7)

---

## Layer 5: The Motifs (Recurring Elements)

Motifs are the connective tissue. They should evolve across chapters — not repeat. If a motif appears the same way twice, it's stalling.

### What to watch for:
- A motif appearing in the same form in two chapters (stalling)
- A motif being explained rather than shown ("The bumblebees are like the data patterns" — if you have to say it, the motif isn't working)
- Too many motifs active in one chapter (dilution)
- A motif promised in the TLDR or soundlayer that never appears in the content

---

## The Seams (Cross-Chapter Check)

After assessing each chapter individually, check the joints between them.

1. **Chapter endings → Chapter beginnings:** Does each chapter's closing physical/emotional state connect to the next chapter's opening? Map them:
   - End of Ch1 → Start of Ch2
   - End of Ch2 → Start of Ch3
   - End of Ch3 → Start of Ch4
   - (etc.)
2. **Escalation:** Does each chapter raise the stakes or deepen the question? If two adjacent chapters make the same point at the same intensity, one of them may be redundant.
3. **The "you" problem:** Track every shift from "I" to "you" across the entire piece. Is "you" used consistently? Does it always mean the same thing (the listener)? Or does it sometimes mean "people in general"?
4. **Factual consistency:** If a detail is introduced in one chapter (e.g., the wall is fifty metres away), does it stay consistent when referenced later?
5. 
6. **Soundlayer vs. Content vs. TLDR:** All three should describe the same chapter. If they diverge, one of them was written at a different stage and hasn't been updated. Identify which is the authority (mostly the content) and align the others.

## Quick-Reference Checklist

For each chapter, confirm:

- [ ] Narrator's location is established in the content (not just metadata)
- [ ] At least one physical/sensory detail in the first three sentences
- [ ] Every intellectual passage has a sensory trigger
- [ ] No case study repeats another's point
- [ ] No editorial punchlines ("None of this is a flaw" type)
- [ ] "I" and "you" usage is intentional, not drifting
- [ ] Every sentence can be spoken aloud in one breath
- [ ] Soundlayer and content agree on what the narrator perceives
- [ ] Soundlayer adds at least one thing the text doesn't say
- [ ] Every motif has evolved from its previous appearance
- [ ] TLDR matches what the content actually delivers
- [ ] The chapter ends in the body (a sensation, a perception) not in the head (a thesis, a summary)
