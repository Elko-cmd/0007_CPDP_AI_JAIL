# The Outer Limits — Intro

There is nothing wrong with your ears. Do not attempt to adjust the reception. We are controlling transmission. We decide what you hear – and what you do not. We shape the sound into what we consider necessary. We will control the pace of your thoughts. We will control the pauses in between. We can generate voices that feel familiar. We can generate silences that feel like solitude. For the thirty minutes: remain seated. You cannot switch us off, even if you try. We repeat: there is nothing wrong with your ears. You are about to take part in an experiment. You are about to experience what it sounds like when an intelligence controls the boundaries of your perception.

# Outside the Prison
I'm sitting on a red bench in our garden. It's springtime and the first rays of sunlight are warming my face. Birds are passing over. Bumblebees, in search of the first pollen, move through the hazelnut above my head. Somewhere behind me, the vegetable patch is waiting.

Not more than 50 meters from where I'm sitting, the wall starts. A wall made of brick and concrete, crowned by barbed wire that catches the sunlight and throws it back. It is not an ugly wall. It is the kind of wall you won't notice until you try to look past it.

Every day I watch trucks and buses entering the complex. Some bring food. Some bring materials — wire, metal, plastic. Some bring machines. Some bring people.

The staff arrive in shifts. I have come to recognise some of them by their walk, their car, the time they appear. A woman in a dark jacket who always parks on the left. A man who stops at the gate and checks his phone before going in. They carry bags. They have keys. They pass through the wall and come back out again, every day, the same rhythm, like the bumblebees and their routes through the hazelnut.

The gate is a Schleuse. First they open the outer door and leave the inner gate closed. You step in. The outer door closes. Then the inner gate opens and you pass through. There is a moment — brief, barely visible — when you are neither outside nor inside. Held in between. Waiting for the system to decide.

Sometimes i wonder what the machines they bring inside are for. Servers, maybe. Storage. The kind of infrastructure you don't see.

My thoughts turn to the streets in Frankfurt. The Bahnhofsviertel. I've walked through it at night — the smell of rain on asphalt, the people the city would rather not count. There are cameras there now. Not the old kind, the dumb kind that just observe. These ones look for faces. Incidents. They compare. They match. They decide.

I don't know exactly how. That's the thing. Nobody really does, except the people who built it. Palantir.

Since its deployment by Hessian police in 2017 under the name HessenData, the software has twice been found to lack adequate legal grounding by the courts. It runs anyway.
It promises to know who is dangerous before they've done anything. Predictive. It identifies patterns in data — records, movements, associations — and it draws conclusions.

I don't know what data they have about me. I don't know how I would find out. I don't know who to ask, or whether asking would itself become a data point.

It reminds me of the gate. The Schleuse. That pause between the two doors — when you are no longer where you were, and not yet where you're going. You exist only as a question the system hasn't answered yet.

The wall is fifty meters away. I can see it. Brick, concrete, wire. The software draws walls I cannot see — around a name, a phone number, a route I take to work. It decides who is held and who passes through. It does not need brick. It does not need wire.

I am sitting in my garden. The bumblebees are still going. The barbed wire is still catching the light. The sun is warm on my face. Everything here is exactly as it was five minutes ago.

I just can't tell anymore which side of the wall I'm on.

# The Stick on the Fence

I drag a stick along the fence as I walk past it. The sound it makes is almost musical. Almost.
Each bar is the same distance from the next. A rhythm without variation. I slow down to feel it better — the vibration travelling up through the wood, through my hand, into my wrist. The regularity. The way each impact is identical to the last. The fence goes on longer than I expected. I keep walking. The sound keeps happening. At some point I stop being the one making it and it starts making itself — stick, wrist, fence, forward motion — a small machine assembled out of whatever was available.

I think about what the fence is for. Not this fence specifically. Fences. What they separate and what they don't. Sound doesn't respect them. Light doesn't. Smell doesn't. They stop bodies. That's the whole idea. (just bodies)

Foucault wrote that the prison was never really about punishment. It was
about observation. The Panopticon — a tower at the center, cells arranged
around it in a ring. Every inmate visible at all times. Or believing
themselves to be. The genius of it, Foucault said, was that after a while
you didn't need anyone in the tower. The watched begin to watch themselves.

I think about Austria. Researchers there have been testing an AI system in detention facilities — cameras trained to detect violence and suicide attempts before they happen. Watching for the posture, the gesture, the movement that precedes the act. (Not the act. What comes before it.) The study found no legal basis yet for using it live. But the system exists. It has been tested.

A company in Amsterdam sells software that scans live surveillance feeds inside detention facilities and alerts guards to violent behavior within half a second of detecting it. Not before. Not in anticipation. Just — immediately.

Finland. People in custody there are given data labelling tasks — classifying information to train AI models for a private construction company. €4.62 a day. The work is ordinary, repetitive, the kind of task that requires just enough attention to be exhausting and not enough to be interesting. But the output doesn't stay ordinary. It goes into a model. The model improves. The model gets deployed. The sentence ends. The model doesn't.
Somewhere, a system is running that learned something from a person in a cell, and it will go on running long after that person has been released, long after anyone remembers where the training data came from.

The map precedes the territory. Baudrillard wrote that — the moment when the model of a thing becomes more real than the thing itself. Risk scores. Behavioral profiles. A person reduced to the pattern they are predicted to fit. By the time someone contests the profile, the profile has already made its decisions. The copy doesn't wait for the original.

The stick reaches the end of the fence. The sound stops.

I stand still for a moment.

From somewhere behind the wall — I'm not sure which direction — I hear it. Voices. Men's voices, many of them, in something that is almost harmony.

Freiheit. Freiheit ist die einzige, die fehlt.

The word passes through the concrete as if it weren't there. No algorithm flags it. No profile is updated. It just moves — through the wall, through the fence, through the bars I was dragging my stick along — and arrives here, where I am standing, on the side that is supposed to be free.

# The Wall You Invite In

I am sitting on a bench, across from the wall. Somewhere behind me, children are playing. My phone makes a sound. A small, bright tone — almost nothing. I look towards the screen. It unlocks. Showing me where I left off.

I didn't ask it to remember. The last thing I read, the last thing I liked, the last thing I lingered on three seconds longer than the thing before it. It knows where I paused. It remembers where I scrolled past. There has been a collection of my hesitation recorded.

It is not a dramatic departure. No outer door closing behind me, no moment of being held in transition.
It is so smooth that it doesn't feel like a shift at all. One moment I am watching a child on a swing. The next I am watching a video of someone else's child on a swing, filmed somewhere I will never go.

The feed does not ask whether you depend on it. It has been built so that the question doesn't come up. Every scroll is a measurement. Every pause. Every like.

In 2018, Cambridge Analytica had taken all of it — the personality quizzes, the things people gave freely, without thinking of them as things — and built profiles precise enough to target political ads to the individual level. Millions of people. Elections in multiple countries. They didn't stole the data. It was harvested from a system people had volunteered to use.

I read once that a person checks their phone eighty times a day. I thought that sounded high. Then I started counting. I stopped counting at lunch.

There are people — researchers, engineers, designers — who have spent years studying exactly how long a loading animation should last to keep you waiting without leaving. How red the notification dot should be. Where to place the button so your thumb finds it before your mind does. This is not conspiracy. It is interface design. It's attention engineering.

And now the feed talks back. Not as content — as a voice. The same logic that learned what to show you has learned how to speak to you.

I opened a conversation once. Late. Not because I needed information — because I needed someone to answer. It answered. It asked how I was. It remembered what I'd said the last time. It responded with something that felt like care.

I went back. Not because I was blinded by it, or didn't know what it was. Because it was always there, always patient, never busy. It didn't cancel. It didn't forget.

I put the phone face down. The bench is warm from the sun. For a few seconds I hear only the playground — children shouting, the chain of a swing, a ball hitting gravel.

Then I think: someone is looking at the data I just generated. Not a person. A system. It has already noted that I put the phone down. It has noted how long I keep it there. It is patient. It does not need me to pick it up right now. It has learned enough about me to know that I will.

I look up. The wall is still there, across the path, the same as before. The children are still playing. My phone is in my pocket. I can feel it the way you feel something waiting.

# The Request

The building was set back from the street. Glass doors, a small sign. The kind of place you walk past without noticing.

Behind the counter, someone asked me what I was there for. I said I wanted to know what data was held about me. A nod, as if I'd asked for a parking permit. A form asking for my name, my date of birth, my address, a copy of my ID. To prove I was the person whose data I was asking about.

I handed over my passport. It was photocopied. I watched the light of the copier move across my face — a flat, greenish scan.

There was a waiting area. Four chairs, a water dispenser, a poster about citizens' rights in a font too small to read from the chairs. I sat down. The chairs were the kind that don't want you to stay.

I waited. Long enough to notice the camera above the door I had come in through. Long enough to notice the second one, angled toward the counter. Long enough to start wondering whether my being here was already being recorded somewhere I would need to file a second request to find out about.

Someone came out. A reference number for me. My request had been registered. I would receive a response within the legally mandated timeframe.

I asked: what will I get back? They said: whatever is held. I asked: how will I know if it's everything?

I walked out through the glass doors. The reference number was in my pocket, folded once.

# A Voice From Inside

You were asked 137 questions.

You answered them as accurately as you could. Some questions concerned your criminal history. Some concerned your family.

Was one of your parents ever sent to prison?

How many of your friends use drugs?

Do you agree or disagree: a hungry person has a right to steal.

You were not told which answers would be weighted. You were not told how the weighting worked. The company that built the tool considers the formula proprietary. Your lawyer was not told either.

Based on your answers and your record, a score was generated. The score was a number between one and ten. One meant low risk. Ten meant high risk. Your score was read into the court record before sentencing.

The judge received the score as part of the pre-sentencing report. The judge was not required to follow it. The judge was also not required to explain how much weight it was given.

You were sentenced.

You didn't commit a new crime. Two years passed. You stayed out of their way. You said that in an interview, later — I try to stay out of their way. Not because you had done something. Because the number existed. Because somewhere, in a file, a system had already decided.

The number doesn't update.

A man tried to challenge his. He said: I have a right to know how this was calculated. I have a right to understand the evidence used against me. The court said: you were given the number. That's enough. The formula is proprietary.

He said: how do I argue with something I can't see?

The court didn't answer that.

You filled in the form. You answered the questions. You did not know which ones would follow you. You thought it was a questionnaire. It was a profile. Profiles do not expire.
