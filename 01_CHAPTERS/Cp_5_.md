# The Request
## Soundlayer

Neither organic nor digital. Institutional. The sound world is a third thing — the building itself.

Opens with the glass door: a mechanical latch, a pneumatic closer, the brief rush of street sound cut off. From here, no birds, no synth, no notification tones. The garden and the phone are both gone. What remains is the architecture: fluorescent hum, ventilation, the acoustic deadness of a room designed to absorb rather than reflect. Every surface says waiting room.

The copier is the chapter's central sound event. A slow mechanical pass — not unlike the stick on the fence in Ch3, but contained, clinical. A machine reading a face. The light-scan sound should be uncomfortably precise: a single sweep, then silence.

During the waiting passage, the water dispenser becomes the only moving thing in the room. An occasional plastic gurgle. The cameras are silent — their presence is felt through what's missing. No ambient life. The room listens without making sound.

When the reference number is delivered, there is no change in the room tone. The system responds at the same volume, the same temperature, as when the narrator entered. Nothing registers the narrator's question. The room doesn't react.

The glass door again on exit — but this time reversed. The pneumatic closer, the latch. No street sound rushes back in. The chapter ends in the dead air between the door closing and the outside arriving. A held breath the building takes.

Arc position:
- Ch4: digital is home, organic interrupts
- **Ch5: neither — institutional. The room replaces both. No garden, no screen. Only process.**
- Ch6: near-silence, someone else's room tone

The soundlayer should feel like the inside of the Schleuse. The narrator has entered the pause between doors. Neither side is audible.

## TLDR:
### Chapter: The Request
The narrator tries to find out what the system knows about them. A GDPR Auskunft — a data access request. They enter an office, a building, a process. The Schleuse is no longer metaphor: they are buzzed in, they wait, they are called. The chapter follows the experience of being processed — forms, reference numbers, the language of rights that sounds like protection but feels like procedure. The narrator asks for their data. The system asks for their ID. The question of who is verifying whom is never resolved.

### Themes:
the right to access, bureaucratic enclosure, GDPR as lived experience vs. legal text, identification as submission, the Schleuse made institutional.

## Content

The building was set back from the street. Glass doors, a small sign. The kind of place you walk past without noticing.

Behind the counter, someone asked me what I was there for. I said I wanted to know what data was held about me. A nod, as if I'd asked for a parking permit. A form asking for my name, my date of birth, my address, a copy of my ID. To prove I was the person whose data I was asking about.

I handed over my passport. It was photocopied. I watched the light of the copier move across my face — a flat, greenish scan.

There was a waiting area. Four chairs, a water dispenser, a poster about citizens' rights in a font too small to read from the chairs. I sat down. The chairs were the kind that don't want you to stay.

I waited. Long enough to notice the camera above the door I had come in through. Long enough to notice the second one, angled toward the counter. Long enough to start wondering whether my being here was already being recorded somewhere I would need to file a second request to find out about.

Someone came out. A reference number for me. My request had been registered. I would receive a response within the legally mandated timeframe.

I asked: what will I get back? They said: whatever is held. I asked: how will I know if it's everything? 

I walked out through the glass doors. The reference number was in my pocket, folded once.
