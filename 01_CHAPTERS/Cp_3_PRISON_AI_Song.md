## TLDR:
### Chapter: The Stick on the Fence
The narrator drags a stick along the prison fence — a rhythm, a grid — and the sound triggers a chain of thought. Foucault's Panopticon is introduced not as theory but as lived feeling: the watched learning to watch themselves. This threads into concrete cases of AI deployment in European prisons — predictive cameras in Austria, real-time violence detection in the Netherlands, prisoners in Finland training AI models for cents a day, phone calls monitored in Belgium for the shape of intent. Baudrillard surfaces underneath: the profile replacing the person, the simulation running without an off switch. The chapter closes with the prison choir singing Freiheit through the wall, and leftist demonstrators singing back from outside — the word passing through concrete in both directions. The narrator picks up the stick again and doesn't know what to do with it.
### Themes: 
discipline and observation (Foucault), simulation and the precession of the model (Baudrillard), AI in carceral systems, labour exploitation, abolition, the porousness of walls.

## Content
I drag a stick along the fence as I walk past it. The sound it makes is almost musical. Almost.
Each bar is the same distance from the next. A rhythm without variation. I slow down to feel it better — the vibration travelling up through the wood, through my hand, into my wrist. The regularity. The way each impact is identical to the last. The fence goes on longer than I expected. I keep walking. The sound keeps happening. At some point I stop being the one making it and it starts making itself — stick, wrist, fence, forward motion — a small machine assembled out of whatever was available.

I think about what the fence is for. Not this fence specifically. Fences. What they separate and what they don't. Sound doesn't respect them. Light doesn't. Smell doesn't. They stop bodies. That's the whole idea. (just bodies)

Foucault wrote that the prison was never really about punishment. It was 
about observation. The Panopticon — a tower at the center, cells arranged 
around it in a ring. Every inmate visible at all times. Or believing 
themselves to be. The genius of it, Foucault said, was that after a while 
you didn't need anyone in the tower. The watched begin to watch themselves.

I think about Austria. Researchers there have been testing an AI system in detention facilities — cameras trained to detect violence and suicide attempts before they happen. Watching for the posture, the gesture, the movement that precedes the act. (Not the act. What comes before it.) The study found no legal basis yet for using it live. But the system exists. It has been tested.

A company in Amsterdam sells software that scans live surveillance feeds inside detention facilities and alerts guards to violent behavior within half a second of detecting it. Not before. Not in anticipation. Just — immediately.

Finland. People in custody there are given data labelling tasks — classifying information to train AI models for a private construction company. €4.62 a day. The work is ordinary, repetitive, the kind of task that requires just enough attention to be exhausting and not enough to be interesting. But the output doesn't stay ordinary. It goes into a model. The model improves. The model gets deployed. The sentence ends. The model doesn't. 
Somewhere, a system is running that learned something from a person in a cell, and it will go on running long after that person has been released, long after anyone remembers where the training data came from.

A person reduced to the pattern they are predicted to fit. By the time someone contests the profile, the profile has already made its decisions. The copy doesn't wait for the original. -->

The stick reaches the end of the fence. The sound stops.

I stand still for a moment.

From somewhere behind the wall — I'm not sure which direction — I hear it. Voices. Men's voices, many of them, in something that is almost harmony.

Freiheit. Freiheit ist die einzige, die fehlt.

The men inside know what is missing. Some of them also know — more precisely than I do, sitting here — what they took from someone else. They have a word for the first thing. They sing it through the concrete. The other word goes unsung.

Out here, the cameras learn the walk. The model holds the profile. The system does not wait to be asked. The same word would apply. No one is singing it yet.

I pick up the stick. I don't know what to do with it.

### Context
Der Song “Freiheit” erschien 1987 auf Westernhagen’s Studioalbum Westernhagen. Als Single-Auskopplung erschien der Song aber erst am 18. September 1990, um das Live-Album Live zu promoten, das zehn Tage nach der Single erschienen ist.

Mitgeschnitten wurde dafür das Konzert, das Westernhagen 1989 in der Dortmunder Westfalenhalle gab. Ebenso wie auf der Studioversion wird Westernhagen nur von einem Klavier begleitet.

Während der Live-Version wird Westernhagen durch lautes Mitsingen des Publikums über den gesamten Song hinweg unterstützt. Die Schlussstrophe,

Alle die von Freiheit träumen / sollten’s Feiern nicht versäumen

bekam zu der Zeit der Live-Aufnahme am 20. Dezember 1989 durch die politischen Ereignisse eine größere Dimension, da erst am 9. November die Grenze zwischen Ost- und West-Deutschland gefallen war.

Obwohl der Song eigentlich den Verlust der Freiheit besingt, wird der Song – neben “Wind of Change” von den Scorpions – als einer der Hymnen zur deutschen Einheit gezählt.
