## Content

You were asked 137 questions.

You answered them as accurately as you could. Some questions concerned your criminal history. Some concerned your family.

Was one of your parents ever sent to prison?

How many of your friends use drugs?

Do you agree or disagree: a hungry person has a right to steal.

None of them concerned anyone you had harmed. The system was not built to ask.

You were not told which answers would be weighted. You were not told how the weighting worked. The company that built the tool considers the formula proprietary. Your lawyer was not told either.

Based on your answers and your record, a score was generated. The score was a number between one and ten. One meant low risk. Ten meant high risk. Your score was read into the court record before sentencing.

The judge received the score as part of the pre-sentencing report. The judge was not required to follow it. The judge was also not required to explain how much weight it was given.

You were sentenced.

You didn't commit a new crime. Two years passed. You stayed out of their way. You said that in an interview, later — I try to stay out of their way. Not because you had done something. Because the number existed. Because somewhere, in a file, a system had already decided.

The number stays in the file.

A man tried to challenge his. He said: I have a right to know how this was calculated. I have a right to understand the evidence used against me. The court said: you were given the number. That's enough. The formula is proprietary.

Studies later showed the formula scored Black defendants as high-risk nearly twice as often as white defendants with comparable records. The company disputed the findings. The formula remained closed.

He said: how do I contest something I can't see?

The court didn't answer.

You filled in the form. You answered the questions. You did not know which ones would follow you. You thought it was a questionnaire. It was a profile. It is still being read aloud.

## Sources

### COMPAS (Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions)
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMPAS_(software)>

Risk assessment software developed by Northpointe (now Equivant) in 1998. Evaluates recidivism risk using criminal history, social environment, drug involvement, and other factors. Generates scores on a 1–10 scale. The algorithm is proprietary — defendants and their lawyers cannot examine how the score is calculated. Used in sentencing, parole, and pretrial decisions across multiple US states.

### State v. Loomis (Wisconsin Supreme Court, 2016)

Eric Loomis challenged his sentence, arguing that the court's use of a proprietary COMPAS risk score violated his due process rights — he could not examine or contest the algorithm used against him. The Wisconsin Supreme Court upheld the sentence, ruling that COMPAS scores could be used in sentencing as long as they were not the sole determining factor. The court required that sentencing judges be given written advisories about the tool's limitations, including that it was not designed for sentencing and may reflect group-level biases. The formula remained proprietary.

### ProPublica — "Machine Bias" (2016)
<https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing>

Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Lauren Kirchner, Surya Mattu. Published May 23, 2016. Investigation analysing COMPAS scores for over 7,000 defendants in Broward County, Florida. Found that Black defendants were nearly twice as likely as white defendants to be falsely flagged as high risk (false positive), while white defendants were more likely to be incorrectly labelled low risk despite going on to reoffend (false negative). Only 20% of people predicted to commit violent crimes actually did. The investigation became the landmark reference for public debate on algorithmic bias in criminal justice.
